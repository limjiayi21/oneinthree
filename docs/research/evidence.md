# One in Three - evidence file

Research compiled and verified on 2026-11-12 for Scenario 1 (a woman walking home alone at night from a New York City subway station; then the same street seen as a bystander). The machine-readable version is `content/evidence.json`; the IDs below match it.

## How to read this file

- **Everything here was opened and checked during this session.** Where a primary document could not be opened (paywall or bot-blocking), the figure is taken from a reputable report of it and the entry says so. Anything that could not be confirmed is listed under [Not verified or deliberately left out](#not-verified-or-deliberately-left-out) and is not in the JSON.
- **Figures are reproduced exactly; wording is paraphrased.** To respect copyright, source sentences are not copied out. Each entry instead gives the exact figure as printed and where it sits in the source (section, key finding, table or page), so it can be checked in seconds. The single direct quotation is the WHO wording behind the product's name.
- **Three kinds of evidence, with very different weight:** nationally representative surveys (Stop Street Harassment/GfK, Gallup, ONS, Pew); self-selected surveys and story archives (Hollaback!/Right To Be, the two New York City transit surveys); and laboratory or vignette studies of rejection and aggression. The entry notes which is which.
- **'1 in 3' is not a street harassment statistic.** It is WHO's lifetime estimate of physical or sexual violence. The closest street-harassment equivalents are 65% (US women, 2014) and 78% (women in eight countries, 2019).

## B. How harassers behave and how they respond to different reactions

**Bottom line.** There is good evidence on what street harassment consists of, who does it, what men say they want from it, and how women respond. There is laboratory evidence that some men react to rejection with hostility. There is **no** study that measures how a street harasser reacts to a specific response from the woman (ignoring, a polite no, a firm no, an insult, an excuse, a threat to call the police, going into a shop). Anything of the form 'she did X, so he does Y with probability p' is an assumption, and the calibration table labels it that way.

## C. Bystander intervention

### The 5Ds (Right To Be)

Descriptions and examples below are faithful paraphrases of Right To Be's guide, not its official wording. Use the linked guide for the official text and ask Right To Be for permission before reproducing it in the product: [Right To Be 2026](https://righttobe.org/guides/bystander-intervention-training/).

| Key | Name | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| `distract` | Distract | An indirect, low-key way to interrupt the incident: ignore the person doing the harassing, engage the person being harassed, and talk about something unrelated. | Ask her for directions or the time, or greet her as if she were a friend you have just bumped into, and stand between the two of them. |
| `delegate` | Delegate | Ask someone else to help: the person next to you or someone with authority in the space. Say clearly what you see and what you want them to do. | Tell a shop worker, bus driver or station agent what is happening and ask them to step in. Do not call the police unless the person being harassed asks you to. |
| `document` | Document | Record or take notes on what is happening, but only if someone else is already helping and you are safe. | Film from a safe distance, then give the footage to the person who was harassed and let them decide what happens to it. Never post it without their permission. |
| `delay` | Delay | If you cannot act in the moment, check in with the person afterwards; it reduces the harm even when the incident is over. | Ask whether she is okay, tell her you saw it and it was not acceptable, and offer to walk with her or sit with her for a while. |
| `direct` | Direct | Name the behaviour or tell the person to stop, briefly and firmly. Use with caution: it is the riskiest option because the person may turn on you or escalate. | Say one short, calm sentence telling him to leave her alone, then turn your attention to her. Do not argue or debate. |

## E. Calibration guidance for the decision model

How to read this table. basis=data means the value is taken directly from a cited figure (the range shows the spread across sources; a single-source figure has a point range). basis=inferred means the value was derived from cited figures collected in a different setting or by combining figures. basis=assumption means no measurement exists and the value is a conservative placeholder; where the rationale says the direction or ordering is evidence-based, only the ordering should be treated as supported.

**Key limits.** (1) No study measures how street harassers react to specific responses, so every response.* and bystander.<tactic>.* value is an assumption except where marked. (2) Incident-type shares come from self-selected story archives (Hollaback!) that over-represent memorable and severe incidents; national surveys give lifetime prevalence, not per-encounter rates. (3) Rejection-aggression experiments involve college men in dating or lab settings, not strangers on a street.

**Design guidance that follows from the evidence.** Let most of the variance come from the man's disposition, which is drawn at the start of a run, and let the trainee's response shift probabilities only modestly; never make a bad outcome the scripted consequence of a particular choice, because the evidence does not support a 'correct' response and guidance is explicit that harassment is not the target's fault; keep physical escalation rare; make friendly engagement prolong contact, insults the most likely to draw hostility, and reaching other people or a shop the most reliable way to end the encounter; in the bystander part, make indirect tactics low-risk and Direct effective but riskier for the bystander.

## What the evidence does NOT tell us

1. **How a harasser reacts to a given response.** No study measures the probability that a street harasser backs off, persists, follows or turns hostile after the woman ignores him, declines politely, sets a firm boundary, insults him, makes an excuse, threatens to call the police or goes into a shop. The strongest material is practitioner guidance and first-person accounts.
2. **Per-encounter base rates.** National surveys report lifetime prevalence (ever followed, ever touched). Incident archives report the mix of incidents that people chose to write up, which over-represents frightening ones. Nobody has measured what share of all street approaches stay verbal, involve following or turn physical.
3. **How encounters end.** There are no figures for how long incidents last or how they stop.
4. **How often rejection leads to violence.** Laboratory studies show that rejection raises hostility in some men, and real cases show the worst outcome exists. Neither tells us the rate on the street. The case studies are illustrations, not base rates.
5. **Which bystander tactic works best.** One New York analysis found harassment more likely to stop when a bystander confronted the harasser, without giving a number. No study compares the 5Ds against each other, and the only evaluation of 5Ds training found was run by its sponsor without a control group.
6. **Whether specific safety habits work.** There is data on how many women hold keys, stay on the phone or change routes, and none on whether these change what a harasser does. No verified figure exists for pretending to be on the phone.
7. **Night versus day.** Fear is much higher after dark, but in the one New York incident sample that recorded time of day, daytime and night-time incidents were about equally common, and a third gave no time.
8. **Representative New York City prevalence.** Both New York transit surveys were open online surveys. Official NYPD or MTA complaint counts were not retrieved, and would under-count in any case given that roughly nine in ten incidents go unreported.
9. **Who harasses.** Survey evidence says harassers are overwhelmingly men, usually alone and usually strangers. The men who have been studied directly are mostly students or self-described occasional catcallers; persistent night-time harassers have not been studied. Nothing here supports portraying the man as any particular race, class or age, and the best-known New York footage was criticised for exactly that bias.
10. **Experiences beyond the scenario.** The US national survey found people of colour, lower-income people and LGBT people disproportionately affected, and that men's most common harassment was homophobic or transphobic slurs. Scenario 1 does not represent those experiences.

## Not verified or deliberately left out

- **Fairchild & Rudman (2008), detailed frequencies and coping percentages.** Only the abstract is open; the full text is paywalled. Only abstract-level findings are used.
- **Stratmoen et al. (2018), original abstract.** The publisher blocked access. The finding is taken from a Psychology Today summary by a psychologist and is marked as secondary.
- **Walton & Pedersen (2021), full text.** The publisher blocked access. The abstract was read through Semantic Scholar and the percentages come from PsyPost's report, which includes an interview with the first author.
- **Manhattan Borough President's 2007 subway report.** The original PDF could not be found online; figures come from a 2007 amNewYork report of the press conference.
- **NYU Rudin Center 'Pink Tax' report (2018).** The PDF blocked automated access; figures come from amNewYork's report, and the NYU landing page was opened to confirm the study and its authors.
- **UN Women UK / APPG report (2021).** The PDF blocked automated access; figures come from The Guardian's corrected article. Do not use the widely repeated 97% figure: it was an error and was corrected to 86%.
- **Outcomes of the Ruth George and Mary Spears cases.** Charges were verified; no opened source confirmed a verdict or sentence, so the summaries stop at the charges.
- **Tiarah Poyau (Brooklyn, 2016).** Considered as a New York City case and left out: search results indicated the defendant was acquitted of murder and convicted of lesser charges, and the account of what prompted the shooting rests on an unnamed police source. Not opened and not reliable enough.
- **Darley & Latane (1968) percentages.** The database record has no abstract and the publisher page did not load, so the classic figures are not quoted. The 2011 meta-analysis is used for the bystander effect instead.
- **Cornell-Hollaback! 2015 US results** (for example 85% first harassed before age 17; 77% of under-40s followed in the past year; 67% first harassed before 14). These were confirmed on Cornell's own news page: [Cornell University ILR School 2015](https://www.ilr.cornell.edu/news/faculty/street-harassment-statistics). They are left out of the statistics because the sample was a self-selected online survey of nearly 4,900 people and the figures would overstate the national picture. The international release is here: [Cornell University ILR School 2015](https://www.ilr.cornell.edu/news/about-ilr/global-research).
- **L'Oreal Paris / Ipsos 2021 wave** (about 80% of women; changes in behaviour such as avoiding places). Seen on Ipsos France and in a later press release, but the two pages give different country counts (14 and 15), so only the fully documented 2019 wave is used.
- **L'Oreal Paris Stand Up training evaluation (2022).** Sponsor-run comparison of trainees with the general public, with no control group. Not used as evidence that training works.
- **Stop Street Harassment's National Street Harassment Hotline (855-897-5910).** Still listed, but the page was last updated in December 2022 and gives reduced hours. Confirm it is operating before showing the number.
- **NYC HOPE (nyc.gov/nychope).** The page blocked access, so it is not listed. Safe Horizon's 24-hour hotline was verified instead.
- **The detail that a man walked beside the woman for five minutes in the 2014 Manhattan video.** Seen only in search snippets, not in an opened article.
- **BBC, AP, Reuters and New York Times coverage of the case studies.** These sites could not be fetched; CBS News (with AP), Al Jazeera (AFP), NBC and local stations were used instead.

Internal inconsistencies noticed in sources: the 2014 US report gives both 86% and 84% for women harassed more than once; the 2011 New York summary prints 13.3% beside counts of 6 out of 99; the Swiss paper gives both 45% never intervening and 43.8% ever intervening without reconciling them. The figures used here are the ones shown.

## How verification was done

- Pages were opened with a web fetch tool or, where a site blocked it, in a browser, and the relevant figures were read from the page text. No access controls or bot checks were bypassed; blocked sources were replaced or dropped.
- PDF reports (the two Stop Street Harassment national reports, the Cornell New York report, the 2011 Hollaback! summary, Hutson & Krueger, Fleetwood) were converted to text and read directly, so those figures were checked against the full documents.
- Journal abstracts were read from PubMed, Springer or Semantic Scholar records. For those papers only abstract-level claims are made, unless stated otherwise.
